# The Persona Gap

Most people carry a gap between the first impression they give and the person who eventually shows up. I have noticed the gap in myself is unusually large — and that people often feel tricked when it closes.

My first impression tends to be calm, composed, serious. Then, as I open up, out comes something else: blunt humor, dark jokes, sudden tangents into philosophy or economics or politics. The reaction is remarkably consistent. People feel deceived, as if the early version was a sales pitch and this is the defective product.

For a long time I assumed I should fix one of the two selves. Either sand down the second one to match the first, or lead with the truth from minute one. Neither feels right. The polished version is not a lie exactly — it is what composure looks like from outside. But it is not the whole story either, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty.

I have come to suspect the real issue is not authenticity at all. It is the cost of suppression. For years I performed normalcy — edited my jokes, my interests, my questions — because fitting in seemed like the price of being accepted. And the performance had a physiological bill: constant self-monitoring, anxiety, overthinking, tension, guilt. Bottling things up is not free; the body keeps collecting.

So at some point I decided to stop paying. Let the tangents happen. Ask "what's the meaning of life? why do we work?" into an awkward lunch silence. Be openly, unapologetically enthusiastic about the things I am enthusiastic about. If the persona gap widens rather than narrows, so be it.

I will admit the strategy has costs. Some people are visibly put off when the composed stranger turns out to be this. I have learned to take those reactions gracefully and even playfully — yes, this is me; no, I never claimed otherwise. Everyone misreads everyone. The gap is not a betrayal; it is just depth revealing itself at the speed of trust.

There is also a subtler point hiding here about credentials and expectations. People sometimes project a certain seriousness onto me based on my background and education, and then experience the reality as a letdown. But that projection was never mine. It was built entirely on their side of the bridge. I did not trick them; their model of me was under-specified.

Is dropping the mask immature? Undisciplined? Maybe. I genuinely do not know. What I know is which state feels livable. Performing normal produced anxiety and a racing heart. Being the whole thing, all at once, produces occasional awkward silences and a few disgusted looks — and, crucially, it filters fast. The people who stay after seeing the full picture are the ones worth having around.

The persona gap is uncomfortable for everyone. But closing it by shrinking yourself is the worse trade.

# Finding Zettelkasten Too Late

Something I hate about life is how late I always learn things. Not things nobody knows — things that have existed for decades, sitting there, waiting, while I reinvented worse versions of them.

The latest example: digital gardens and [[Zettelkasten]]. I only discovered them days ago, through a conversation with [[ChatGPT]]. I had been drowning in information for years — collecting constantly, retaining almost nothing — and the recommendation to tend a digital garden sounded trivial. I thought I had heard of Zettelkasten before. I had not actually implemented it until today.

It is making learning fun again. That is the part that stings. The method is not complicated; it is just linking notes instead of hoarding them. And the [[Opportunity Cost]] of not knowing it sooner is enormous when I add it up: all the hours spent babysitting scattered notes in whatever app I was using that month, managing consumption instead of compounding it. A different organizational habit, adopted years earlier, would have changed how much of what I read actually stayed with me. Some of it contributed to [[fail in college]].

And it is never just note-taking. Nobody taught me nutrition either — how to read a label, compare value per price, notice what food does over years. Nobody teaches you the boring infrastructure of life; you find out late or never. I try to forgive my parents for that. It is their first life too. They passed along what they had, and what they had did not include these maps.

But here is the uncomfortable twist: I do not think an early warning would have worked anyway. If someone had told fifteen-year-old me "link your notes, eat your vegetables," I would have nodded and ignored it. That is the pattern — we walk toward a hole while someone shouts "there's a hole ahead," fall in anyway, and then mumble from the bottom, "if only I'd listened." We seem to need the consequences personally before advice becomes information.

That is why humans keep repeating the same mistakes, generation after generation. History repeats not because we forget but because knowledge that arrives as advice is weightless; only experience has mass. Each of us feels our own story is unique, but structurally it is mostly a rerun — a linear combination of everything that happened to the people before us.

Even this reflection has that shape. Part of me enjoys gardening notes because it gives me something to tell people, which embarrasses me — we are social animals, starved for [[Social Proof]], and I cannot pretend otherwise. Why do I want to be known so badly? Why can't I just focus on myself? No answer yet.

Maybe this, too, is just the human condition rather than my personal flaw. We are cursed to need validation, cursed to distrust secondhand warnings, cursed to learn everything the expensive way. It is hard to trust advice; trusting costs something, and falling into holes is at least familiar. This might simply be the fitrah of being human — the nature we are born with, holes included.

At least now the notes link to each other.

# Crying at the Halting Problem

I don't remember which day it was — yesterday, maybe the day before. I was wandering through my own memory the way you wander the internet: no destination, just association. Open Instagram, check the explore page, see a clip from a drama, and somehow that recalls a quote about cats and neocortices. Follow the quote, follow a thread, and suddenly I'm reading about Gödel's incompleteness theorem.

Which reminded me of something else entirely: a lecturer standing in front of my class years ago, not talking about Gödel but about the halting problem — Turing's proof that there is no general procedure which can look at any program and any input and tell you whether it ever finishes running. This time I actually looked up the explanation. Roger Penrose, apparently, hates what the theorem implies about minds and machines.

And when I read it, I cried.

It's hard to explain why. There's a feeling of knowing something is real — really, undeniably right — while being unable to prove it, or even to say properly what "it" is. The halting problem has that shape: you can state the proof in five lines, but what it *means* — that there are truths no procedure reaches, questions that stay open forever not because we're too dumb but because they're built that way — hits somewhere below argument. So yes, I was probably carried away by feeling more than by logic.

Here's the part that sobered me up afterward. Any theorem is true only within the system it's proven in; step outside the axioms and it means nothing. Maybe the whole thing is bullshit and I got emotional over notation. I try to be careful with myself about exactly this. When I look at something and find a pattern that delights me, that delight is often a trap: you see beauty in something, and only you can see it. And things whose beauty is visible to an audience of one are usually not beauty at all — they're apophenia, the mind's habit of finding faces in static. Most of what deserves the word "true" is validated in common, by other people checking each other's work. Private conviction, however luminous, doesn't get to vote.

So there are two honest readings of that evening. One: I brushed against a genuine limit — the strange comfort of a proof that says some doors have no keys — and my body responded before my reason could. Two: my pattern-matcher fired on random input, Instagram to kdrama to cats to Gödel, and mistook the dopamine of connection for discovery. Both readings fit the same facts. That ambiguity is itself the lesson: the feeling of certainty is data, but it isn't proof — and the halting problem, of all things, should teach us the difference.

I stopped writing when it came time for maghrib. Some thoughts are better closed at sunset anyway.

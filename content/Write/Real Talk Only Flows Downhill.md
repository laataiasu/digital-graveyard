# Real Talk Only Flows Downhill

There is a strange asymmetry in conversation that I have only recently been able to name: honest talk seems to flow in only one direction — downward. Between people of unequal standing, rawness has to be granted from above. It is rarely safe to offer it from below.

Think about what honesty actually costs. To say something real to someone, you need a guarantee they will not use it against you. It is not really about reputation; it is about opportunity. Every candid sentence is a small exposure of your flank, and the person with less power has more flank to expose.

This is why I dread team outings and forced social events. The conversation never gets anywhere substantial. Everyone performs. The topics stay at whatever level the average person in the room can accept, which means anyone who wants to be understood has to lower their intellectual register until it matches. Bringing something beyond the room's grasp feels almost rude — you become either a bore or a clown.

The dynamics get worse as the power gap widens. A meaningful conversation between a superior and a subordinate can usually only be initiated by the one on top. If the junior initiates a hard topic, he risks looking like a jester playing above his station. But even when the senior initiates it, something odd happens: what should read as vulnerability often reads instead as weakness, and most people cannot catch the hidden pattern, so it gets misread anyway. And no matter how much the initiator explains, the audience cannot fully relate, because they lack the experiences that made the thought necessary in the first place.

So everyone converges on safe ground. Not because anyone is dishonest, but because the structure of the situation punishes anything else.

Which leads me to a conclusion that sounds paradoxical but does not feel that way from inside: the internet is more real than the real world. Online, the mask produces honesty rather than preventing it. Anonymity removes exactly the thing that makes offline candor expensive — the risk of losing face, standing, or future opportunities. When nobody knows who you are, you can finally show who you are. The mask is what lets the truest self speak.

This is part of why I feel drawn to decentralized, pseudonymous spaces like crypto communities. People share things raw, unfiltered, without a professional persona bolted on. Yes, the same mask attracts clowns and scammers — anonymity cuts both ways. But my favorite counterexample is someone like 0xngmi, who built DefiLlama: an anonymous builder who made something genuinely useful, harmed no one, and made money doing it. No brand, no face, just the work.

That model appeals to me more than any career path I can see from where I stand. I would love to build alongside people like that, or become one myself. I do not envy him — envy would miss the point. What I feel is closer to orientation: evidence that a certain kind of honest, useful existence is possible.

I still have not settled on the one thing I should commit to building. But I know more clearly now what disqualifies a life: one where every conversation is calibrated to the power differential, where nothing real is ever said out loud. Real talk only flows downhill. I want to live somewhere gravity does not apply.

# The Utilitarian Trap and Human Friction

When you look at the world purely through an engineering or business lens, everything reduces to input and output. The dogma is deceptively clean: minimize cost, maximize throughput, compress latency. In that mental framework, the process itself holds zero inherent value. It does not matter whether a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice.

This utilitarian mindset makes the adoption of [[Artificial Intelligence]] feel like a moral and practical imperative. Why spend hours combining disparate visual textures, agonizing over color weights, or iterating manually when a model can synthesize an output in seconds? To the utilitarian, craft looks like inefficiency disguised as romanticism. The machine represents an asymptotic march toward perfection.

Yet when you sit across from someone who actually makes things — someone who touches the materials, who understands the visceral friction of composing something out of raw human intention — that sterile confidence starts to fracture.

The counter-argument from practitioners is not nostalgia; it is an aesthetic and existential diagnosis. Generative systems produce an average of the collective latent space: competent, polished, and profoundly bland. What makes human creation resonate is precisely the idiosyncratic friction that algorithms seek to optimize away. The imperfections, the tedious merging of unexpected elements, the stubborn refusal to take the shortest path — that is where taste lives.

Total optimization has a darker downstream consequence: it erodes the capacity to appreciate anything that is simply *alive*. 

When you train yourself to care only about outcomes, you gradually lose patience for the messiness of living. You grow intolerant of waiting and being waited for. You begin to view everyday human rituals — culinary curiosity, fashion, cultural quirks, the desire to explore places without a KPI — as irrational noise. You retreat into a detachment that calls itself rationalism, but is actually an aversion to friction.

The paradox of isolation is that it protects you from the discomfort of compromise, but at the expense of vitality. Life does not happen in an optimized, friction-free vacuum. It exists in the contrast, in the tension between competing energies, and in the willingness to let something un-optimized touch you without immediately trying to engineer it into submission.

Perhaps the ultimate risk of the utilitarian trap is not that the machines will fail, but that we will succeed in turning ourselves into them — efficient, frictionless, and entirely hollow.
